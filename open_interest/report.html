China futures open interest and return forecast recurrence report

Reproduction paper: Harrison Hong & Motohiro Yogo，《What Does Futures Market Interest Tell Us about the Macroeconomy and Asset Prices?》，Journal of Financial Economics, 2012 (The full text is based on the revised version of NBER Working Paper 16712) · China Commodity Futures 2010-03 to 2026-09, 199 months

The paper's two main findings are consistent but statistically weaker in China: commodity open-interest notional growth predicts higher commodity returns (+0.47 (1.75) per 1 standard deviation, paper +0.72% (t=2.47)), and higher basis (more expensive deferred-contract) lower subsequent returns (-0.82 (-2.54), paper -0.57% (t=−2.08)). The predictive power of positions is concentrated after 2016; it disappears when only the number of contracts is used; past returns have no predictive power. The prediction direction for national bonds is consistent with the paper but not significant, and it has no predictive power for stock indexes.

What does the paper talk about

The traditional view is that futures prices already contain all information about the future economy and asset prices, and open interest is irrelevant. The paper believes that when the economy is booming, hedging demand increases (producers sell futures and consumers buy futures), so open interest rises with the boom; however, the direction of hedging is uncertain, and prices may rise or fall. Coupled with the slow diffusion of information and limited arbitrage,Open interest is a more reliable signal of prosperity than price, can predict commodity returns, and can also predict bond returns (prosperity → inflationary pressure → bond decline).

1. Position growth and commodity income

The average monthly return of the commodity portfolio is 0.15%, with a monthly fluctuation of 3.55%; the first-order autocorrelation of position growth is 0.82, and the correlation with the basis is 0.17. In the paper, holdings have almost no correlation with basis and are positively correlated with returns, and the same is true for China.

  • Basis: The higher the basis (the deferred-contract is more expensive than the nearby-contract, that is, the premium), the lower the subsequent commodity returns are, significantly, consistent with the paper and previous carry research.
  • Position growth: The direction is consistent with the paper, and it is close to significance after adding basis; it is significant after 2016, and the effect is stronger after controlling for past returns.
  • past earningsThere is no predictive power (same as the paper), even negative in the second half.
  • Only use the number of contractsPosition growth has no predictive power, and the paper states that the results of the two calibers are similar. China's predictive power seems to rely on the price component of open-interest notional, which weakens the explanation that "open interest itself contains information".

The paper finds that growth in commodity holdings significantly predicts lower Treasury yields (−0.32% per 1 standard deviation), and growth in bond holdings also predicts lower bond returns. The direction is the same for China, but the sample of Treasury futures is only about 11 years, which is not significant. The growth of stock index futures positions has no predictive power, and the stock market has the weakest evidence in the paper.

Conclusion

  • The main conclusions of the paper are consistent in China: rising positions indicate higher commodity returns, and high basis indicates lower returns.
  • But the evidence is weak: the full sample is only close to significance, and is only significant after 2016; it disappears when only the number of contracts is used, indicating that the information may mainly come from the price rather than the position itself.
  • The macro explanation of the paper (positions rise with the economy, predicting inflation and bond decline) requires interest rate and economic activity data, which could not be tested this time.

Differences from the paper

  • The full text is based on the revised version of the NBER working paper (August 2011), and the officially published version may be subject to change.
  • Short-term interest rates, term spreads, economic activity index and CFTC hedger positions are missing, and the corresponding control regressions have not been performed.
  • Sample 2010–2026 (approximately 16 years), Papers 1965–2008.
  • The basis is calculated using the nearest contract instead of spot.
