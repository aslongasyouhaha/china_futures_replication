Does Time Series Momentum Really Exist: A Recurrence Report

Reproduction paper: Dashan Huang, Jiangyuan Li, Liyao Wang & Guofu Zhou，《Time Series Momentum: Is It There?》，Journal of Financial Economics, 2020 (the full text is based on the author's accepted manuscript) · China Futures 58 instruments (commodities, stock indexes, government bonds) with more than 5 years of data, 2005–2026 · Monthly

The statistical conclusions of the paper generally hold true in China:Looking at instrument by instrument, earnings over the past 12 months are barely predictive of the next month.(Only 2 of the 58 instruments are significantly positive, and 33 have negative slopes); the t value of the pooled regression is 2.28, which drops to 1.12 after controlling for instrument fixed effects, and is only 0.91 after 2016. But the economic conclusion of the paper is in ChinaNot established: The paper says that TSM makes money only because it buys assets with high long-term averages, which performs the same as "buying and selling at historical averages" (TSH); in China, TSH basically makes no money (equal-weighted monthly average -0.03%), TSM averages 0.26% per month, the difference between the two is 0.29% (p = 0.12), and TSM's predictions are obviously not like TSH's predictions. In addition, the bootstrapping method used in the paper to deny significance is flawed: it resamples observations one by one, destroying the correlation between assets in the same month, and the critical value is systematically raised. After switching to monthly block resampling, the TSM for the full sample is significant at the 5% level (one-sided p = 0.016), but not significant when controlling for fixed effects or looking only at 2016 onwards.

What does the paper talk about

Moskowitz, Ooi & Using a pooled regression of 55 futures (all assets stacked together), Pedersen (2012) finds that past 12-month returns predict next month returns (t ≈ 4), calling it “time series momentum everywhere”. This paper uses the same data to question: ① Asset-by-asset regression, most of which are not significant, out-of-sample is almost not as good as the historical mean; ② The t value of the pooled regression is overestimated - the mean value of each asset is different, the predictor variable is highly persistent, and volatility scaling will make it too large. The critical values (12.53, 4.83) obtained by the bootstrap method are all higher than the true t value of 4.34; ③ TSM Strategies make money, but a strategy that doesn't require any predictability - long-short the sign of an asset's historical mean (TSH) - performs almost identically, because assets that have risen over the past 12 months tend to be the ones with the high long-term mean.

Why is the critical value of paper-style specification so large?

The wild bootstrap method in the paper multiplies each observation (instrument × month) independently by ±1, and the pairs bootstrap method resamples each observation one by one. Both approaches areDestroys the correlation between asset returns within the same month: In the simulated sample, the clustering standard error becomes very small, and the t value is enlarged accordingly. So the distribution of simulated t is far to the right of the real t (past 12 months for this sample: real t 2.28, wild simulated 97.5% quantile 7.30), and using it as a critical value will almost certainly result in "not significant". The 12.53 vs. 4.34 of the paper is the same phenomenon. In addition, the paper generates samples based on the estimated model (β = β̂) rather than the null hypothesis (β = 0). This distribution is inherently centered on the true t and is not suitable for testing β = 0.

Whole block redrawing on a monthly basis (all assets in the same month are drawn together and multiplied together by ±1) preserves the cross-sectional correlation, and the critical value returns to around 2 (wild 2.03, pairs 2.14). Follow this correct approach:

  • Full sample trailing 12-month returns: t = 2.28, monthly block p = 0.018 (wild)/0.016 (pairs), significant at the 5% level; signed version t = 2.49.
  • The significance comes almost entirely from commodities (t = 2.21) and before 2016 (t = 2.12); the stock index and treasury bonds are not significant, and after 2016 t = 0.91.
  • After controlling for instrument fixed effects, t = 1.12, without volatility scaling, t = 0.58, both are not significant: consistent with the argument of the paper, mean difference and volatility scaling raise the t of pooled regression.
  • The results for one month lagged in China (Panel A) are different from those in the paper: the return for the month lagged 12 months is significantly negative (t ≈ −2.8), which is close to 0 in the paper.

Consistent with the paper: when β = 0.1, TSM and TSH are indistinguishable, and when β = 0.4, TSM almost always wins significantly. In China's real data, TSM is 0.29% higher than TSH per month, which is close to the level of β ≈ 0.2~0.4 in the simulation; however, β estimated by fixed effects is negative, and the two are inconsistent. This shows that TSM's excess returns do not come from the stable linear relationship of "the same instrument rose in the past and will continue to rise in the future", but is more likely to come from the trend of a small number of instruments and a small number of periods (commodities before 2016).

Conclusion

  • Statistically, the conclusion of the paper is basically valid in China: There is almost no predictability on an instrument-by-instrument basis; the significance of pooled regression mainly comes from the mean difference and volatility scaling between instruments, which disappears after controlling for fixed effects; it is not significant after 2016. This is consistent with the "post-2016 Sharpe only 0.24" in this library's TSMOM recurrence.
  • The essay’s self-help approach is flawed: Observation-by-observation resampling destroys cross-sectional correlation and critical values are systematically raised.
