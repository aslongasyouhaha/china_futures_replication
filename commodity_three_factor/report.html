China commodity futures three-factor model recurrence report

Recurrence paper: Gurdip Bakshi, Xiaohui Gao & Alberto G. Rossi，《Understanding the Sources of Risk Underlying the Cross Section of Commodity Returns》，Management Science, 2019 · China Commodity Futures 2006–2026 · Excluding transaction costs (same paper)

Reproduced according to the method of the paper, the results of Chinese commodities are basically consistent with the paper: the carry and momentum factor returns are both significant (Sharpe 0.83 and 1.06, the paper 0.73 and 0.61). If any one of them is removed, the model will be rejected. Both are necessary to explain the cross-sectional returns of commodities. However, the economic explanation given in the paper (carry corresponds to stock market volatility risk, momentum corresponds to speculative activities) cannot be supported in China. After switching to the dominant contract with concentrated transactions in the Chinese market, carry is still significant, but the momentum factor constructed according to the paper's method (6-month look back) is no longer significant. The reason is that the price trend of Chinese commodities is mainly reflected in the nearby contract approaching delivery; if we look at 12 months, the "momentum" on the dominant contract is significant, but most of it overlaps with the carry of the past year, and the remaining price momentum after deduction is not significant.

  • Carry and stock market volatility: The paper finds that carry falls when stock market volatility rises, and can use this to price carry portfolios. There is no relationship between the two in the Chinese data (t≈0), and pricing is also rejected. Note that this is only the Chinese stock market (since 2010), not the global stock market of the paper.
  • Momentum and Speculation: The paper finds that momentum increases when speculative positions increase. When China’s proxy for speculation (volume ÷ open interest) rises, momentum falls instead (t=−2.3), and the momentum portfolio cannot be priced either. The volume/opening ratio does not have the same meaning as the CFTC's non-commercial holdings, but is only a crude proxy.
  • Commodity volatility cannot price carry portfolios, consistent with the paper.

5. Members of long and short legs and market status

The paper emphasizes that carry is very different from instruments held by momentum (there is only one 75% percentile of instruments selected in the same direction every month). In China, an average of 4.4 (out of 10) are selected in the same direction every month, and 5 at the 75% percentile: instruments with discounts are often instruments that have risen much recently, and the two strategies overlap significantly more.

Part 2: Switch to the dominant contract used by the Chinese market

The second nearby contract held by the paper method is the dominant contract in only 43% of cases in China, and the median trading volume is only 43% of the main contract; the trading of many instruments (iron ore, rebar, soybean meal, PTA, etc.) is concentrated on the January, May, and September contracts. So we use the most liquid dominant contract to calculate it again: the income is used as the main continuous contract, the term structure is used as the annualized price difference between the dominant contract and the more distant active contract (the cross-sectional ranking correlation median of the two term structures is 0.41, the proportion of discount/premium judgments is consistent 70%), the momentum is used as the dominant contract's increase in the past 6 months; the rest are exactly the same.

What can predict nearby-contract excess?past price increases, rather than the current discount level: for an instrument that has risen much in the past, its nearby contract will rise a lot more than the dominant contract next month. A possible explanation (not directly verified, we do not have spot and inventory data): the nearby contract will be delivered immediately and must follow the spot, reflecting the "current" supply and demand; the dominant contract will be delivered in a few months, reflecting the market's expectations for "by then". When commodities continue to rise due to tight supply, the market expects that the tension will ease, and the dominant contract rises with some reservations; however, the current tension often lasts longer than expected, and the near-contract continues to be pulled upwards. Therefore, the price trend of Chinese commodities is mainly reflected at the front end of the curve. Carry is also stronger when using nearby-contract, mainly because instruments with deep discounts are often instruments that have risen much recently.

What’s in the 12-month “momentum”?The post-resumption increase of the main continuous contract consists of two parts: the change in the contract price itself (change in the unreinstated price), and the roll returns brought about by the price difference between the old and new contracts every time the month is changed (the discounted instrument is switched to a cheaper deferred-contract, and each time the month is changed, a little bit is "earned", that is, carry). Let’s use a completely independent test: directly use the contract spread at the end of each month to calculate the average discount over the past 12 months (carry 1-12, without going through price increases or decreases). The 12-month momentum is correlated with its cross-section at 0.48; the momentum strategy annualizes 8.0% (t=2.57), and only 3.3% (t=1.13) remains after regression of the carry1-12 strategy, which is no longer significant.

On the dominant contract, most of the 12-month "momentum" returns overlap with carry over the past year; the remaining price momentum after deducting carry is very weak and not statistically significant (but it cannot be concluded to be zero).The intuition is: for instruments whose supply continues to be tight, prices are rising, and futures are always at a discount. The discount continues to rise continuously through month changes, so "it has risen much in the past" and "it has always been at a discount in the past" are often the same batch of instruments.

About 80% of TSMOM's income from commodities comes from the cross-sectional part, and the part that determines the direction of the entire commodity market is not significant. The cross-sectional part is also significant when looking back at 6 months, which is not inconsistent with the above table: it places bets according to the up and down signs and is not normalized. The positions in months with differentiated instrument trends (half up, half down) are large, and the positions in months with the same ups and downs are almost zero. The 6-month cross-sectional momentum happens to exist only in the months of divergence: 8.3% annualized in months with large divergence (t=2.6) and 2.6% in months with small divergence (t=0.7). The paper's M5 stakes the same size every month, and the effect is diluted. TSMOM also uses the roll-adjusted increase, so a large part of its cross-section of commodities is carry.

Conclusion

  • According to the method of the paper, the conclusion is basically established: Carry (Sharpe 0.83) and momentum (1.06) are both significant, and any model is rejected if removed, consistent with the paper; the fit of the three-factor model is not as good as the paper (R²(GLS) 50%, χ² test marginal rejection).
  • Economic explanation does not hold: Stock market volatility cannot explain carry, and speculation cannot explain momentum; China's carry and momentum both follow the commodity cycle, rather than performing well in recessions as in the paper.
  • Replace with dominant contract: carry is still true (Sharpe 0.69); the momentum constructed according to the paper method (6-month lookback) is no longer significant, no matter how the combination is constructed. The reason is that the 6-month price trend of Chinese commodities is mainly reflected in the nearby contract that is approaching delivery. Looking back at the 12-month period, the "momentum" on the dominant contract is significant, but most of the returns overlap with the carry of the past year, and the remaining price momentum after deduction is not significant; a large part of TSMOM's cross-sectional returns on commodities is also due to this.
  • Momentum under the paper method also contains roll returns: It is calculated based on the income of rolling holdings of nearby-contracts, and also includes the roll returns of changing months. Under the paper's method, how much of the independence of momentum comes from the price trend and how much comes from the roll returns needs to be separated separately (not done).
  • availability: For a nearby-contract, one month before delivery, individual customers must close their positions before the delivery month. Liquidity and capacity are limited, and delivery-related squeezes will also amplify fluctuations; the higher returns under the thesis method are difficult for ordinary investors to obtain.

Differences from the paper

  • A nearby-contract must meet minimum liquidity (open interest ≥ 500 lots and transactions). In the early days, there were often less than 10 qualified instruments. The pricing test was available for 208 months (501 months in the paper), and the test efficiency was even lower.
  • There are 11 test combinations: there is no livestock sector, and the energy and chemical industry is excluded because the near-contract is inactive.
  • The number of instruments per month increased from 10 to more than 60. The fixed number of 5 long and 5 short only covered a small part of both ends in the later period.
  • The economic variables are all proxies: the CSI 300 stands in for global stocks, the volume/opening ratio stands in for CFTC positions, and the state of commodity markets stands in for recessionary periods.
  • No robustness tests such as two-step GMM elimination test and J test, management combination, random grouping, individual instrument test, value factor and downside risk factor have been performed.
